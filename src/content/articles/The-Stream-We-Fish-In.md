“You must live in the present, launch yourself on every wave, find your eternity in each moment.” - Henry David Thoreau

Thoreau spent two years, two months and two days at Walden Pond, turning a philosophy of presence into a daily practice: a morning swim, hours of quiet observation, working his bean field, listening closely to the woods. “Time is but the stream I go a-fishing in,” he said.

There is a kind of freedom in realizing that the present is the only place we ever live and create. Even our memories of the past and our hopes for the future are experienced in the now.

That made me wonder: what does physics say about “now”? Would quantum mechanics and relativity have an explanation for time in the way Thoreau perceived it? This article is an exploration of why our experience of the present still remains something we have to live, moment by moment.

## Introduction

Recently, I have been contemplating how we only ever experience the present. Everything we remember that happened in our past was always a present moment. Whatever we will experience in the future will also be experienced in the present moment. This does not mean that every moment we experience is the same. It means that, wherever we are in our lives, we are always experiencing them here and now, not in the past or in the future.

Yet the world around us keeps changing. Trees grow, we age, and familiar objects wear down. A clock measures elapsed time, but each of us experiences its passage differently. How does this ever-present “now” relate to time in physics? Having studied and written about quantum mechanics for some time, this question drew me to it as a place where I might find some answers, even though they may not be definitive ones. Quantum mechanics offers another way to think about time and change where classical interpretations leave questions unanswered.

## Quantum Mechanics and Change

In ordinary non-relativistic quantum mechanics, time is usually a parameter: a value that tells us when we are describing a system. A quantum state is the mathematical description used to calculate the probabilities of measurement results. The theory tells us how that state develops from one time to another, but the time variable is not itself a description of how we perceive “now.” This distinction gives us a starting point for exploring the connection between quantum mechanics and our experience of time. An equation can describe how a particle’s state changes from one time to another, yet leave open the question of why we experience each moment as the present.

Schrödinger’s equation provides a framework for the interaction between time and the change in the state of a system. It uses the Hamiltonian, the operator representing the system’s energy, to determine how the quantum state evolves. Knowing the initial state and the Hamiltonian lets us calculate the later state of an isolated system, even though individual measurement results can remain uncertain. It would be misleading to say simply that more energy makes everything change faster. Using a time-independent Hamiltonian, a state with one definite energy can evolve while the probabilities of its measurement remain unchanged. Quantum mechanics does not simply remove time, it provides a distinction between how a state evolves through time and what we know about a particular measurement outcome.

## Relativity and the Present

Relativity complicates our perception of the present. Our own sense of “now” might seem to suggest it is a single moment shared by the whole universe. Observers moving relative to one another can disagree about whether distant events happen at the same time, and clocks can record different elapsed times along different paths through spacetime. In general relativity, matter and energy also shape spacetime itself. The present we experience locally cannot simply be applied into a universal “now.”

One way to think about this spacetime picture is the block universe. In this view, events we call past, present, and future all exist simultaneously in a four-dimensional structure of spacetime. In the block universe, spacetime is viewed as a static, unchanging “block” containing three spatial dimensions and one time dimension. It suggests that there is no objective “flow” of time, and that our feeling of the flow of time is simply a subjective experience shaped by our consciousness moving along the dimension of time.

## Conclusion

The trees continue to grow, we grow older, and familiar objects bear the marks of time. Yet we meet each change in the present, with memories of what has been and thoughts of what may come. Quantum mechanics offers a way to describe change, while the question of how we experience it remains open. Perhaps this exploration leaves us not with a final answer, but with a reason to contemplate what it means to belong to a universe described across time, while living it one moment at a time. By paying closer attention to the present, we may each find our own unique way of understanding our place within that universe.
