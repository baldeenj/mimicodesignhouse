# Introduction

Quantum computing has advanced rapidly in recent years, moving from a largely theoretical idea in laboratories to early applications across multiple industries. These advances have sparked debate in the blockchain community about whether quantum machines could threaten cryptocurrencies. In principle, quantum computers can solve certain hard mathematical problems much faster than classical computers. These problems underpin how cryptocurrency transactions are secured and verified. Meanwhile, today’s Bitcoin network is powered by roughly fifteen gigawatts of electricity, exceeding the electricity use of many countries. That scale of energy and hardware ensures that no single miner can find a valid block header—the structure miners vary to produce proof of work—faster than brute force allows.

Miners do this by trying billions of random values (called nonces) until one, when hashed with the block header, produces an output below the network’s difficulty target. On average, they must make N guesses, where N is the size of the search space (about 2^70 hashes). Grover’s algorithm is often presented as a way to change this game: in an idealized setting it offers a quadratic speedup for unstructured search. A perfect quantum computer running Grover’s algorithm would reduce the expected work from about 2^70 trials to about 2^35 trials \[1].

This raises the question of whether quantum computers could “break Bitcoin mining.” Although Grover’s algorithm looks powerful on paper, using it for Bitcoin would require massive fault-tolerant hardware, fully reversible SHA‑256 circuits, and a staggering amount of energy. And even if such a machine were built, Bitcoin’s difficulty adjustment would respond to faster block discovery and quickly erase any gained advantage. The more important long-term question is not mining speed, but how quantum computers could weaken the cryptography that protects ownership and transactions.

# What quantum computing changes

Grover’s algorithm provides a quadratic advantage for unstructured search: it can find a marked item in a set of size N using on the order of √N oracle queries. In cryptographic terms, this effectively reduces n-bit preimage resistance to about n/2 bits against an ideal quantum attacker. Applied to Bitcoin mining, that sounds like it could “halve the exponent” in the brute-force hunt for a block hash below the difficulty target. However, Grover’s speedup only materializes if the hash function can be implemented as a reversible quantum oracle—an additional circuit layer that must be executed with fault-tolerance.

Today’s quantum devices are noisy due to qubit decoherence and gate failures, so practical algorithms require error correction. With surface-code error correction, a single reliable logical qubit can require thousands (or more) physical qubits, plus constant classical processing to decode errors. That overhead turns a conceptually simple search into an enormous engineering problem. Prior estimates suggest that doing just one SHA‑256 hash inside a fully error‑corrected quantum computer would take roughly 300,000 basic low‑level operations. For Bitcoin’s full double‑SHA‑256, this translates into about 1011× more low-level work than the naïve Grover oracle-count estimate would suggest, so the apparent quadratic speedup is largely swallowed by fault-tolerant overhead.

A Grover-capable miner would not behave exactly like a classical miner. Because Grover search is run in coherent “rounds,” a quantum miner may choose to stop and measure as soon as a rival block is announced, creating tighter races and increasing the chance of forks. Some analyses also note that a strategically placed miner could try to exploit timestamp flexibility to influence the next difficulty adjustment, temporarily raising rivals’ costs while relying on its own (temporary) quantum edge. Bitcoin retargets difficulty every 2,016 blocks (about two weeks) to maintain a \~10‑minute block interval; if blocks arrive faster than expected, the protocol raises difficulty, and if they arrive slower, it lowers it \[2]. As a result, even a miner that could find blocks 10× faster would, within one retarget period, face roughly 10× higher difficulty, thus erasing any sustained advantage and returning miners to competition for the same fixed block reward.

Ultimately, all these scenarios depend on building and operating large, fault‑tolerant quantum computers. Recent work argues that quadratic speedups like Grover’s rarely beat well-optimized classical methods once error correction, gate synthesis, and realistic clock speeds are accounted for. In other words, the crossover point where fault‑tolerant quantum search would overtake brute force appears far beyond near-term device capabilities. Even under optimistic assumptions, quadratic quantum speedups for problems like the shortest‑vector problem (often discussed in the context of lattice-based, post‑quantum signatures) remain impractical at meaningful security levels. Taken together, these results reinforce a broader theme: quadratic speedups often do not survive the full cost of fault‑tolerant implementation.

# Stellar power requirements

Large-scale resource estimates are dominated by uncertainty in power draw per qubit. Published projections span milliwatts to watts per qubit for fault‑tolerant systems, and the total is driven less by the algorithm than by the supporting infrastructure required to keep vast numbers of qubits stable. Major contributors include cryogenic cooling (for superconducting devices), control and readout electronics, continual classical decoding for surface-code error correction, and the distillation “factories” that supply the high-quality states needed to run a reversible double‑SHA‑256 circuit. When these per-qubit loads are multiplied by the number of physical qubits implied by fault tolerance, the aggregate demand can quickly grow from data center scale to grid scale.

In the resource-estimation framework used in recent analyses, the mining target is parameterized by difficulty bits b: smaller b corresponds to an easier search (more acceptable hashes), whereas larger b corresponds to the tighter target observed on Bitcoin’s live network (mainnet), which automatically adjusts difficulty over time. Even in an unusually favourable partial preimage case (e.g., b = 32, corresponding to 2224 acceptable outputs), a superconducting, surface-code mining fleet is estimated to require on the order of 108 physical qubits and roughly 104 MW of power, an amount already comparable to the output of a large national grid.

At a target closer to Bitcoin’s January 2025 mainnet level (reported in the same analysis as b ≈ 79), the implied requirements become extreme: about 1023 physical qubits and about 1025 W, approaching Kardashev Type II (stellar) energy scales. The underlying reason is that miners must complete enough Grover iterations within the \~10‑minute block interval to win the block race; as b increases, both the required number of iterations and the fault‑tolerant overhead (e.g., larger code distances, higher distillation throughput, and greater parallelism) increase. Consequently, total qubit count and power demand can grow superlinearly, rather than following Grover’s ideal square‑root scaling.

# Mining is not the main threat

Proof-of-work mining is unlikely to be Bitcoin’s primary quantum vulnerability. When the full physical costs of Grover-based mining are accounted for, especially energy and hardware scale, the result is not a practical shortcut but an impractically large machine. A simple way to see this is to compare network power requirements at Bitcoin’s January 2025 mainnet difficulty: the classical mining network draws roughly PCl ≈ 10–15 GW, whereas a superconducting, fault‑tolerant quantum mining fleet is estimated to require PQ ≈ 1025 W. The ratio PQ/PCl ≈ 1014 indicates that quantum mining would need about fourteen orders of magnitude of improvement in energy and hardware efficiency just to match today’s classical energy footprint, before it could compete on cost.

By contrast, Shor’s algorithm targets the digital signatures that control who can spend coins. Bitcoin uses the ECDSA signature scheme (on the secp256k1 curve); a sufficiently powerful, error‑corrected quantum computer could, in principle, derive a private key from a revealed public key and then create a valid-looking signature for an unauthorized spend. The risk is highest when a public key is already visible on-chain (for example, after an address has been used before), because many modern address formats reveal only a hash until the moment of spending. In the most acute scenario, broadcasting a transaction briefly exposes the public key, creating a short race. This allows an attacker to try to compute the key and publish a competing transaction before the original transaction is confirmed. This can be mitigated by avoiding address reuse, moving funds from outputs that expose public keys, and migrating to quantum‑resistant signature schemes.

# Conclusion

Quantum computing does change the theoretical landscape for Bitcoin, but the most important impacts are not where popular narratives often place them. Grover’s algorithm can reduce the number of trials needed for an unstructured search, yet turning that advantage into a real-world mining edge requires fully error‑corrected hardware, reversible hashing circuits, and extensive supporting infrastructure. Once these costs are included, and once Bitcoin’s built-in difficulty retargeting is considered, the prospect of “quantum mining” at mainnet difficulty collapses into requirements that range from national-grid power levels in favourable toy settings to effectively stellar energy scales at realistic parameters. In short, faster search alone is not a practical path to dominating proof-of-work.

The more credible long-term concern is Shor’s algorithm applied to Bitcoin’s signature scheme: if large fault‑tolerant quantum computers arrive, they could enable private-key recovery from revealed public keys and thus unauthorized spends, especially for outputs whose public keys are already exposed on-chain or revealed during spending. This threat model points to practical priorities today: minimize public-key exposure by avoiding address reuse, migrate funds away from legacy scripts that reveal keys, and support protocol and wallet upgrades toward quantum‑resistant signature schemes. Bitcoin’s security has always evolved through conservative, multi-year engineering; the appropriate response to quantum progress is therefore neither panic nor complacency, but steady preparation focused on signatures rather than mining.
