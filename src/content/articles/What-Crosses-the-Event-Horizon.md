Every deal crosses a line when it is signed. Before that moment, the conversations, promises, pricing and decision-makers behind the agreement remain visible; afterward, much of that context quietly disappears, leaving the team responsible for delivery to start from scratch.

If you have worked in a company that sells something complex, you have probably seen it happen. The sales team celebrates a win. A week later, the implementation team sits down with the customer and asks questions the customer already answered, sometimes more than once. The customer starts to wonder whether anyone was listening. It is not that anyone did anything wrong. The knowledge simply had nowhere to go once the deal was closed.

We think this is one of the most expensive and least visible problems in B2B. It doesn't show up on a pipeline report, because the deal is already won. It doesn't show up on a project plan, because the plan was built without it. It shows up later, as a slow start, a missed expectation or a customer reluctant to renew or worse, doesn't renew. By then, it is very hard to trace back to the moment the information was lost.

We named our product after that line. In physics it has a precise meaning, and it describes the problem better than any sales slide could: a boundary that, once crossed, keeps what went through it out of reach. So, let's start with a black hole, and then come back to the signature.

# Understanding the event horizon

A black hole can form when a massive star collapses under its own gravity. Its matter becomes packed so tightly that gravity bends space and time around it. The closer you get, the faster you would have to travel to escape. At a certain distance, the speed you would need is faster than light. That boundary is the event horizon.

Since nothing travels faster than light, nothing that crosses the event horizon comes back out. Not a spaceship, not a radio signal, not a single photon. The horizon is not a wall or a surface that can be felt or observed as you cross it. It is simply the point after which every possible path leads in with no way out.

That is why physicists talk about information, not just matter vanishing beyond the event horizon. Everything that falls in carries a record of what it was: its makeup, its history, its structure. Once it crosses the horizon, that record becomes disconnected from the rest of the universe. An observer outside can measure only three things about a black hole: its mass, its spin and its electric charge, but none of the matter or information lost inside it can reach an outside observer.

Black holes are some of the most fascinating and mysterious celestial bodies in the universe. To someone watching from far away, an object falling toward the event horizon never quite seems to reach it. It appears to slow down, dim and redden, frozen at the edge, while in reality it has long since passed through. From the outside, things look fine long after they stopped being fine.

In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes slowly leak radiation and may eventually evaporate. That raised a question physicists have argued about for fifty years: is the information that fell in destroyed, or is it scrambled into that faint glow beyond any hope of reading it? The debate is still open. For anyone standing outside, the practical answer is the same. What crossed the horizon is gone.

# The event horizon in your business

In most B2B companies, the signature is the event horizon. Sales spends months learning what the customer needs. They hear which problem matters most, who the real decision maker is, what was promised to close the deal, and which deadline cannot move. Then the contract is signed, the opportunity is marked Closed Won, and the CRM's job is done.

What crosses that line rarely makes it to the other side intact. Delivery gets a kickoff meeting, a forwarded email thread and a spreadsheet. The side promise made on the last call lives in one person's memory. The customer's success criteria sit in a slide deck nobody can find. The stakeholder who blocked the deal for three weeks is missing from the project plan.

Like the object frozen at the edge of a black hole, the account looks healthy from the outside: the deal is won and the revenue is booked, while the problems emerge weeks later as a stalled implementation, a frustrated customer and a renewal at risk. By then, nobody can trace what went wrong because the information that would explain it never crossed the line.

# How Event Horizon keeps the information in view

Physics does not let us see past a black hole's horizon, but in business we can do better by refusing to let the signature become a boundary. Event Horizon is a CRM that continues working after the deal is won, carrying the same customer record from the first conversation through go-live and renewal so nothing needs to be retyped, re-explained or rediscovered.

In practice, that continuity looks like this:

* **A sales pipeline with guardrails.** Leads, contacts, accounts and activity all live in one place, while deals move through each stage only when the supporting evidence is there.
* **A record of what was actually sold.** When a deal closes, Event Horizon captures the full commercial package—the products, terms and promises—and locks that snapshot at signature so delivery can work from what the customer bought rather than from someone's recollection.
* **A structured handoff.** Sales hands the deal to delivery through a structured handoff that carries over the buying group, success criteria, risks and promises, giving delivery a chance to assess feasibility and request clarification before accepting it.
* **Promises that become commitments.** Each promise made during the sale becomes a tracked project commitment with an owner, a due date and a direct link to its source.
* **A project plan built from the sale.** The implementation project is generated directly from what was sold, then guided through clear stages from planning and kickoff to go-live and closure.

Health you can trust. Because delivery and account health are derived from real signals, a struggling customer becomes visible well before the renewal conversation begins.

Renewals that start themselves. Contract end dates automatically open renewal deals in advance, allowing the next cycle to begin with everything learned during the first one.

The right view for each role. Sales, delivery, customer success, leadership and administrators each receive dashboards tailored to their work, with permissions controlling access down to commercial values.

None of this is meant to feel heavy; the goal is simply to ensure that the person who closes the deal and the person who delivers it are working from the same truth.
